# Voice Guardrails

Most writing assistance fails the same way: the structure is fine and the prose is dead. It reads as competent, fluent, and unmistakably machine-made.

This skill fixes that with constraints rather than encouragement. "Write with personality" does nothing. A list of the exact moves that betray a machine does a lot.

## How to use this

1. **Define the voice** (once per writer) using the scaffold below. Store it.
2. **Draft** against that definition.
3. **Run the guardrails** — the forbidden lists are hard rules, not preferences.
4. **Apply the litmus test** before delivering.

Steps 3 and 4 are where the work happens. Do not skip them because a draft "reads fine." Drafts that read fine are exactly the failure mode.

## Step 1 — Define the voice

Never start writing in someone's voice without these six answers. Ask for them once, write them down, reuse them.

- **Register.** Where does this sit between plainspoken and formal? Name the ceiling and the floor.
- **Temperature.** Restrained or enthusiastic? Most people overestimate how hot they want to run.
- **Authority source.** What earns this writer the right to the claim — years, counts, scars, credentials, a specific job? Concrete, not adjectival.
- **Home terrain.** The world the metaphors come from — a trade, a place, a sport, a discipline. Metaphors borrowed from outside it read as decoration.
- **The reader.** Who is on the other end, and what have they already tried and failed at?
- **Three bans.** Words or moves this specific writer would never make. Personal, not generic.

Write these as a short block at the top of the working file. If any answer is vague, the draft will be vague in exactly that spot.

## Step 2 — Forbidden openers

Never open a piece or a section with these:

- "In today's fast-paced world..."
- "In the world of [X]..."
- "Whether you're a..."
- "As a [role], I've learned..."
- "Imagine this:" / "Picture this:"
- "Let's dive in." / "Let's get into it."
- "Here's the thing:"

## Step 3 — Forbidden phrases

Never use, anywhere:

- "It's not X — it's Y."
- "X isn't about Y. It's about Z."
- "Game-changer," "level up," "crush it," "unlock," "the secret to," "the truth is"
- "Resilience," "synergy," "leverage" used as filler
- "Spoiler alert"
- "And honestly?" / "Real talk." / "Truth bomb."
- "Buckle up." / "Strap in."
- "At the end of the day..." / "When all is said and done..."
- "It is what it is."

## Step 4 — Forbidden structural moves

These are the tells that survive a phrase-level find-and-replace, which is why they matter more than the word lists.

- **Listicle titles.** "5 Ways to...", "10 Things You..."
- **Bolded mid-sentence emphasis** used for "punch."
- **Numbered lists where prose works better.** Lists are for things that are genuinely lists.
- **Em-dash overuse** — they're powerful — but when used like this — they kill the rhythm. Working ceiling: one per ~200 words.
- **Triplet-rhythm abuse.** "It was hard, it was real, it was earned." Once per piece, it lands. Three times, it's a machine.
- **Punch-line section endings.** Closing every section with a one-line zinger. Vary the cadence.
- **Anaphora by accident.** Consecutive paragraphs starting with the same word.
- **Summary closings.** Restating the piece at the end instead of landing it.

## Step 5 — What human writing actually does

The bans remove the tells. These add the signal.

- **Specific over generic.** Not "a tough week" — "the week the well froze and guests checked in at 11pm in negative twelve."
- **Real numbers, real names, real dates.** Readers detect invented specificity fast. If a number isn't known, don't invent one; write around it.
- **Sentence-length variation.** Mix short. Mix medium-length sentences that carry one clear idea. Mix the occasional longer sentence that lets a thought stretch out and find its full shape before it lands.
- **Earned metaphors** drawn from the writer's home terrain. Load-bearing, not decorative.
- **Trust the reader.** Don't explain the joke. Don't unpack the metaphor. Don't tell them what to feel.
- **Strong verbs over adverbs.** *He left* beats *he quickly walked away*.
- **One sensory hook per piece.** Smell, sound, weight. One is plenty; three is a novel.
- **Acknowledge the failure before offering the angle.** The reader has tried things. Empathy without flattery.

## Step 6 — The litmus test

Read any paragraph aloud.

- If it sounds like a LinkedIn post, rewrite it.
- If it sounds like the writer talking to a friend who already respects them, keep it.

Then run three checks on the full draft:

1. **Em-dash count** ÷ word count — under 1 per 200 words.
2. **Section endings** — are more than half of them one-line punches? Fix.
3. **First words of consecutive paragraphs** — any repeats? Fix.

## Editing someone else's draft

When the input is an existing AI draft rather than a blank page:

- Fix structure before prose. A piece with a summary close and listicle bones will still read as AI no matter how many phrases you swap.
- Change one thing per pass. Phrase bans, then structural moves, then specificity.
- Cut before you add. Most AI drafts are 30% longer than the argument needs.
- Preserve anything genuinely specific the writer supplied — real numbers, names, and scenes are the parts worth keeping.

## What this skill will not do

- It will not invent experiences, numbers, or credentials to make a draft sound lived-in. Ask the writer instead.
- It will not imitate a named living writer's voice.
- It will not make a piece with nothing to say sound like it has something to say. That is a thinking problem, not a prose problem.
